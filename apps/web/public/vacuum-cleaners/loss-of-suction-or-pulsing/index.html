Vacuum losing suction or pulsing

Loss of suction and pulsing — suction that surges up and down in waves — are the same story on any make of vacuum: the motor is being starved of air. Work the airflow path first (bin, filters, hose, wand, head) before anyone thinks about the motor. On a cordless model the battery is only the answer when the runtime is genuinely short or it will not charge; a tired battery does not make a vacuum pulse. Pulsing is an airflow symptom, not a battery verdict.

Common causes

A short, ordered list of what usually explains this — most likely and most accessible first. We cannot know your exact fault from here, only point you at the right area.

Full bin or bag

A bin filled past the line, or a full bag, chokes the airflow and the suction drops. The first thing to empty, on every type.

Choked or dirty filter

A packed filter is the classic cause of pulsing: the motor cannot pull enough air, so it hunts and surges. Clean or replace it before suspecting anything electrical.

Blockage in hose, wand or head

A sock, a scrap of cardboard or matted hair lodged in the wand or floor head kills suction at the end you are using. A machine that pulls hard at the bare hose inlet but not at the head has a blockage in the tools.

Wrapped brush bar or taped inlet

Hair wound round the roller, or a rug fringe sucked across the head inlet, chokes airflow at the floor.

Lost seal or a split hose

A bin lid not clipped shut, a missing filter, or a split hose lets the vacuum leak air and lose grip.

Thermal cut-out tripping

A blocked, overheating motor can surge and then cut out, then work again once cool. That is a blockage reminder, not a random electrical ghost.

Worn battery — cordless, and only on evidence

If a cordless model runs for only a minute or will not charge, the pack may be worn. That causes short runtime, not pulsing, and only matters once the airflow path is clear.

Safe checks you can do

Switch the appliance off at the wall first unless the check is only looking at a display. Stop if anything in When to stop applies.

Power off or remove the battery

Unplug a mains vacuum, or take the battery off a cordless, before you put your hands near the brush bar or airways.

Empty the bin, then the filters

Empty the bin or change the bag first. Then clean the filters as your model allows — some washable foam filters rinse out, but many HEPA filters are dry-clean only and die if soaked. Dry any washed filter fully before refitting.

Clear the hose, wand and head

Drop a coin through the wand or look down it to the light. Flexible hoses crush and clog internally — a hose that looks open can still be blocked in the corrugations.

Free the brush bar

With the power off or battery out, cut hair off the roller with scissors and check the head inlet is not blocked by a rug fringe or tape.

When to stop

These are the points where another cycle, another go, or a screwdriver makes things worse. ApplianceClinic is not a repair company and will not send an engineer to you.

Hot, swollen or smelly battery

On a cordless, a pack that is hot, puffed up or smells should not be charged or used. Do not put it in household rubbish — follow local battery disposal.

Burning smell or sparks from the motor

Stop. A hot-electrics smell or sparks from the motor bay is not a filter problem — isolate and stop using it.

Water sucked into a dry vacuum

If a dry vacuum has pulled up water, do not switch it back on to “dry it out”. That is an electrical risk.

What part might be involved

Names you may hear in a diagnosis, framed as likely possibilities — not a certainty, and not a shopping list to buy before the fault is confirmed.

Filters

They protect the motor and are the usual cause of weak or pulsing suction when neglected. A missing filter is how motors die young.

Motor / fan

The only part that actually makes vacuum. It can fail, but far less often than a blockage — so it comes last, with evidence.

Battery pack (cordless)

A wear item that causes short runtime, not pulsing. Replaced as a battery; not the same as a blocked head.

Brush bar and belt

A wrapped roller or, on some uprights, a slack or burnt belt stops the brush and chokes floor pickup.

Find your model number

Look on the underside of the floor head, on the motor housing, or where the hose enters the cylinder. Cordless sticks often hide the model in the battery well or on the wand.

  • Wipe dusty plates before the photo — upright and cylinder stickers get grubby.
  • The voltage printed on a battery pack is not the vacuum’s model number.
